Introduction

Theoretically, it is entirely possible that the evolution of life, including intelligent forms of life, has already occurred and concluded around other stars long before our Sun formed. The age of our galaxy, the Milky Way, has allowed enough time for older stellar and planetary systems to form, in which worlds with their own life histories could have emerged, evolved, and disappeared. Evidence of these potential worlds may be forever lost, either through destructive astrophysical processes or through sheer inaccessibility across space and time.

Current estimates suggest that there are approximately 10²² to 2 × 10²³ stars in the observable universe. To put this into perspective: the lower estimate of 10²² stars corresponds to about 1.25 trillion human populations, each consisting of approximately 8 billion people. The upper estimate of 2 × 10²³ stars would correspond to about 25 trillion such populations.

In addition, many older generations of massive stars have already existed and ended their lives. Some collapsed into neutron stars or black holes, others exploded as supernovae, while less massive stars ended as white dwarfs. Through these processes, interstellar space was enriched with heavier elements, the raw material for new star systems, planets, and even ourselves.

Hypothetically, if each galaxy hosted, on average, one civilization, intelligent life would be a common feature of the universe rather than a rare anomaly, although this remains unconfirmed. In this view, the visible cosmic structure of galaxies, stars, and planets is not merely a physical formation, but a potential carrier of consciousness. Considering that the number of galaxies ranges from about 200 billion to 2 trillion, the possible number of civilizations could be of the same order of magnitude, with each galaxy serving as a potential incubator for biological, technological, and other forms of complexity.

Yet such abundance does not guarantee contact: vast distances, relativistic time effects, different stages of development, and the finite speed of light create formidable barriers to information exchange. The likely result is that the isolation of civilizations in space and time is the rule, not the exception.

Within this broader cosmic framework, this calculator offers a structured and explicitly conditional way of estimating how many potentially Earth-like planets may exist in a selected galaxy under a transparent, user-adjustable multiplicative habitability model. It does not claim that such planets are known to exist in those numbers, nor does it directly estimate the number of communicative civilizations. Instead, it examines how astrophysical, geophysical, biochemical, and speculative filters compound under uncertainty.

In this sense, the model is inspired by Drake-style reasoning, but is narrower in scope: it is concerned primarily with the possible occurrence of Earth-like worlds, not with confirming the actual prevalence of intelligence, technology, or interstellar communication. Its purpose is therefore exploratory and comparative rather than predictive in any absolute sense.

By combining observationally constrained parameters with more weakly constrained planetary and biological conditions, the calculator functions as a quantitative tool for probing the boundaries of probability, or reasoned plausibility. It is best understood not as a machine for producing certainty, but as a disciplined framework for asking what follows if particular assumptions are accepted.

About the Calculator

The calculator combines astrophysical parameters, biological or geophysical plausibility filters, and optional speculative factors into a single multiplicative estimate of the number of Earth-like planets. Some parameters are observationally constrained, others are only weakly constrained, and some are explicitly hypothetical.

Inputs can be treated either as fixed point estimates or as uncertain quantities with literature-inspired minimum and maximum bounds. Optional factors such as surface water, CHNOPS availability, complex life, and a wildcard factor can be included or excluded from the calculation.

Observational Priors and Scenario Presets

The calculator includes two observational parameter epochs: Pre-JWST (≤2021) and Post-JWST (2022~2025). These mainly affect observational priors such as the rocky-planet fraction and habitable-zone occurrence, while the scenario cards load broader literature-style combinations of assumptions.

These should be interpreted as parameter presets, not as a formal Bayesian posterior inference engine. In addition to the epoch toggle, the calculator provides named scenario presets such as Consensus, Optimist, Pessimist, and JWST, each paired with a short provenance note so the user can see which literature framing or interpretive stance is being loaded.

Stochastic Approach (Monte Carlo)

The Monte Carlo simulation estimates how uncertainty propagates through the full multiplicative model. For each enabled parameter, the simulation samples values according to the user-selected distribution: log-normal, normal, or uniform. The default engine is standard Monte Carlo, with optional Latin Hypercube sampling for more even coverage of the parameter space.

When no interval is defined, the parameter remains fixed at its mean value. The current model also includes an optional heuristic correlation scaffold between selected parameters, or it can be run in an independent-factor mode. In particular, larger suitable-size values slightly increase the sampled magnetic-field term, and higher lunar-stability values slightly increase the sampled tilt term when the heuristic mode is active. In addition to the output distribution itself, the calculator can now report a convergence diagnostic for the running mean, a parameterwise robust interval envelope, and a sensitivity summary highlighting the strongest positive and negative log-sensitivity drivers.

Distance Estimation ("Where is Everyone?")

After a Monte Carlo run, the calculator estimates the expected distance to the nearest Earth-like planet using a Poisson nearest-neighbor framework under selectable spatial assumptions: a 2D GHZ annulus, a 3D GHZ disk, and a 3D shell-style reference model.

These distance estimates are derived from the estimated number density of Earth-like planets under the selected geometry. When custom galaxy settings are enabled, the geometry can be rescaled to alternative galaxy presets or user-defined diameters and thicknesses. For external galaxies, the current implementation still uses the Earth-to-galaxy value as a reference distance rather than estimating the nearest planet inside that target galaxy, and the Fermi contextualisation panel can be viewed in either Monte Carlo or deterministic mode for comparison.

Output Data

The calculator reports the deterministic point estimate, the Monte Carlo mean, the empirical 95% interval based on q2.5~q97.5, and the simulation-output charts. When relevant, it also surfaces configuration alerts, nearest-distance estimates, and catalogue-based Fermi contextualisation.

The output area combines the histogram and empirical kernel density estimate (KDE) with convergence diagnostics, top-driver sensitivity summaries, and model-health warnings for edge cases such as disabled distance models or normalized host fractions. These visualizations and notices are intended to help users interpret the result, not just read a single headline number.

When Could These Planets Have Formed? · Galaxy History Model (Lineweaver 2004 · Madau & Dickinson 2014)
What this shows 〰 in plain terms: The galaxy is about 13.5 billion years old 〰 almost three times the age of our Sun. This chart shows how the population of potentially habitable planets grew over that vast stretch of time. The answer is far from uniform.

In the very early galaxy, rocky Earth-like planets simply could not exist. The heavy elements needed to build them 〰 iron, silicon, magnesium, carbon 〰 had not yet been forged inside stars and scattered by supernovae. The universe started almost entirely as hydrogen and helium. Only after billions of years of stellar generations did enough "cosmic ingredients" accumulate for rocky planets to form.

Red region (left) = pre-metallicity era. Too early. Rocky planets could not yet form in sufficient numbers.
Green region (right) = biological window. Enough heavy elements exist; conditions are suitable.
Yellow dashed line = when our Sun was born (~8.9 billion years after the Big Bang).

The key insight: A significant fraction of potentially habitable planets formed before our Solar System even existed. If life arose on any of them, it had a multi-billion-year head start on us. This makes the silence we observe in the universe all the more striking 〰 and all the more in need of explanation. The curve models star formation rate × metallicity build-up. It does not predict the number of civilisations 〰 only the physical opportunity window for Earth-like planet formation.
Could We Actually Detect Them? · Detection Horizon & Timing Filter
The problem in plain terms: The calculator tells you how many Earth-like planets may exist. But existence is not the same as detectability. Even if a million civilisations are out there, most of them are invisible to us 〰 either too far away, or transmitting at the wrong time in history.

There are two separate barriers working against detection:

1 · The distance barrier. Signals travel at the speed of light 〰 1 light-year per year. If a civilisation is 10,000 light-years away, their signal needs 10,000 years to reach us. So we can only hear from civilisations within a certain range, determined by how long they have been transmitting. That range is called the detection horizon.

2 · The timing barrier. Even a civilisation within range is only detectable if it happens to be transmitting right now. The galaxy is 13.5 billion years old. If a civilisation transmits for only 1,000 years and then goes quiet, the odds that their broadcast window overlaps with ours today is roughly 1,000 ÷ 13,500,000,000 〰 about one in thirteen million.

Both barriers multiply together. A civilisation must be close enough and transmitting at the same time as us. This is why the detectable number is often drastically smaller than the raw count of habitable planets.

One more assumption is needed: the calculator itself estimates Earth-like planets, not confirmed transmitting societies. The f_tx control below is an explicit conversion factor: the share of Earth-like planets you assume eventually produce a detectable transmitter. Setting f_tx = 1 is an intentionally optimistic upper bound.

How to use the controls below: Set L to your assumed detectable-lifetime in years and f_tx to your civilisation/transmitter prior. Try different values 〰 both assumptions have a very large effect on the answer.
